# The grammar of reading

A short reference to where the language you are learning differs from the languages you already know — and where it does not. It is the Bridge Method applied to grammar: only the points that trip a reader, each one stated the same way in all six languages, with the forms side by side.

It is for learners who know more than one of Immersive's languages, so it is off until you turn it on: **Profile → Account → Languages → Use the languages I know to explain the one I'm learning**.

---

## The Bridge Method

Immersive's six languages — Portuguese, Spanish, Catalan, Italian, French and English — are close relatives. If you read one of them, you already hold a large part of the others: thousands of shared words, the same constructions, parallel endings. The Bridge Method is the idea behind Immersive: rather than teaching the language you are learning from zero, every part of the app starts from the languages you know and stretches them.

In practice it comes down to three things:

- **Comprehension first.** You succeed by understanding a text, not by translating every word, and approximating is allowed. Understanding runs ahead of speaking and writing, so Immersive tracks the two separately.
- **What you know is a resource.** Word aids show the look-alike in a language you know, the reader points out only what is opaque *to you* given your languages, and the aids thin out as your level rises.
- **Grammar as comparison.** The grammar of reading is the method applied to grammar: the constructions that trip a reader, each stated the same way in all six languages, the forms side by side in the order of the Romance continuum (Portuguese, Spanish, Catalan, Italian, French, then English at the French edge), so that each column is a small step from its neighbour.

## Browsing by section

Open **Grammar** and choose the **Grammar of reading** row at the foot of the overview (or go to `/grammar/reading`). The 26 sections are listed by topic under seven headings — lexicon, sound and spelling, syntax, the verb system, determiners, function words, morphology. Each section shows how many comparisons it has, how many **differ from your languages**, and how many work **like yours**.

Open a section to see its comparisons. Each row names the languages that differ, and says either **Differs from yours** or **Same as** the language of yours that works the same way. Rows marked **Forms table** are pure conjugation tables and open in Verbs instead.

## Reading a comparison

A comparison page has three parts:

1. **Which languages differ**, and how this comparison stands against yours. When one of your languages works the same way as the language you are learning, the page quotes the shared form, so you can see the similarity rather than take it on trust.
2. **The statement**, in the language you read best. Use **Read in** to switch it to any of the six; your choice is remembered.
3. **The examples**: the same sentences in all six languages, one row per language in the order of the Romance continuum (Portuguese, Spanish, Catalan, Italian, French, English). A dotted underline marks the language that differs; your own languages and the one you are learning are in full ink.

Below, **See also** lists the related comparisons, and **Learn this properly** links to the grammar notes with exercises that teach the construction.

## In your reading

When a sentence in a document contains one of these constructions, the reader gives it a **dotted underline**. Tap it: the popover shows the translation, then the comparison's summary in your reading language, which of your languages works the same way if one does, the example in your languages, and **Open the comparison**. The comparison page brings you back to the same sentence.

Every published comparison is underlined, including the ones where a language you know already works like the one you are learning: the popover then says so (*Same as Spanish*), which is the reassurance, not a gap. The underlines appear only with the cross-language comparisons turned on in your profile.
